## Quick Facts

This is for golfers playing in Japan who speak little or no Japanese and want to get through booking calls, front-desk check-in, and on-course communication without confusion. No special gear needed — a phone with a translation app and this list is enough. Time to learn the essentials: 30–45 minutes before your trip, plus a printed or saved cheat sheet for the round itself. Relevant year-round, but especially useful in peak spring and autumn weeks when courses are busier and staff have less time to slow down for you.

## Who This Guide Is For

Good fit: golfers booking through phone calls, walk-in reservations, or Japanese-only booking sites; anyone playing at a club without English-speaking staff; groups using a caddie who doesn't speak English. Skip this if you're only playing resort courses in major international hotel chains where English service is standard, or if you're traveling with a Japanese-speaking companion who will handle communication. In those cases, focus your prep time on course selection instead.

## How It Works — Steps

1. Save a core phrase list to your phone's notes app or a physical card — don't rely on internet access at the course, since cell coverage can be weak in mountain or coastal areas.
2. Learn the booking basics first: "Yoyaku onegaishimasu" (reservation, please), "Nan-mei sama desu ka" (how many people — staff will ask this), and numbers one through four in Japanese, since tee times are usually confirmed by headcount.
3. Learn check-in phrases: your name, "Yoyaku shimashita" (I have a reservation), and "Kore desu" (this one) while pointing at a confirmation email or booking screenshot on your phone.
4. Learn caddie and course phrases: "Onegaishimasu" (please/go ahead) before each shot area, "Arigatou gozaimasu" (thank you) after help with distance or club selection, and "Sumimasen" (excuse me / sorry) for slow play or minor mistakes.
5. Practice pronunciation with a translation app's audio playback, not just text — Japanese golf staff are used to hearing accented Japanese, but garbled pronunciation of key words like course names causes real mix-ups.
6. Bring a visual backup: screenshots of your tee time, hotel address, and course name in Japanese characters, since reading kanji aloud is harder than pointing at it.

## Costs & Booking Reality

Learning phrases costs nothing, but the payoff shows up in avoided problems: mistaken tee times, missed cart or caddie add-ons, or confusion over cash-only payment at rural clubhouses. Confirm live, every time: whether the course accepts credit cards or is cash-only, whether caddie fees are mandatory or optional, and whether rental clubs or shoes are available in foreign sizes. Many clubs use phone-only booking or Japanese-language reservation portals — a translation app or a hotel concierge call is often the only way in for non-Japanese speakers. If a course lists an English booking form, double check with the club directly that the tee time actually confirmed, since some third-party sites show availability that isn't current.

## Regional / Course Notes

Private and semi-private clubs, especially older ones inland and in rural prefectures, are the least likely to have English-speaking staff — this is where phrase prep matters most. Resort courses in areas built for international golf tourism (parts of Okinawa, Hokkaido, and large resort operators) more commonly have English signage and staff, though front-desk English fluency still varies by shift. Caddie-mandatory courses, common outside major cities, mean you'll be communicating directly with one person for four-plus hours — a short list of course-specific phrases (yardage, green reading, "left" and "right") makes that time much smoother than gestures alone.

## Common Mistakes

- Assuming a big-name or well-reviewed course automatically means English service — confirm this when booking, not on arrival.
- Relying only on a phone translation app with no offline backup, then losing signal at the course.
- Skipping numbers and counting phrases — group size and tee time slots are almost always confirmed verbally in Japanese.
- Not saving the course name and address in Japanese characters, which makes taxi rides and check-in desks harder than they need to be.
- Overusing English loudly instead of a few correct Japanese words — staff generally respond better to short, correct Japanese phrases than repeated English.
- Forgetting caddie-specific etiquette phrases, which matter more than general small talk during the round.

## Bottom Line

If you're booking directly with Japanese clubs or playing with a caddie, spend 30–45 minutes learning the core reservation, check-in, and on-course phrases above — it prevents the most common trip friction. If you're strictly playing English-staffed resort courses with a Japanese-speaking companion, this prep is optional; put the time into course logistics instead.
